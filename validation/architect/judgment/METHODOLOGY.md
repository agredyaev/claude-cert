# Architect learning and judgment methodology

## Purpose

This workspace prepares for **CCAR-F → CCAR-P** by building decision skill, not by
maximizing exposure to question text. A training item is useful only when it makes
the learner identify the governing constraints, select the best action, explain the
choice, compare plausible alternatives, and transfer the rule to another case.

## Evidence basis

Question construction follows the general one-best-answer principles in the NBME
Item-Writing Guide:

- the lead-in should be closed and focused;
- the item should be answerable conceptually before reading the options;
- options should be homogeneous and judged on one decision axis;
- the key must be the best answer under the stated facts, not merely a true fact;
- distractors should be plausible enough to discriminate between levels of understanding;
- option wording must not leak the key through length, grammatical fit, absolutes, or
  lexical copying from the stem.

Learning flow uses evidence-supported practices summarized by APA, AERO, and IES:

- retrieval practice before restudy;
- explanatory, timely feedback;
- self-explanation before rationale reveal;
- spacing and interleaving;
- varied contexts to improve transfer;
- low-stakes diagnosis for choosing what to study;
- held-out readiness items so mastery is not measured only on familiar questions.

## Judgment item model

A strong architecture item has six parts:

1. **Decision context** — enough system context to understand what is being decided.
2. **Governing factors** — usually 1–3 facts that should change the answer:
   reversibility, risk, latency, cost, uncertainty, scope, permissions, provenance,
   coordination overhead, lifecycle phase, or stakeholder requirement.
3. **Closed lead-in** — asks for the best decision, next action, diagnosis, or design.
4. **Homogeneous alternatives** — all options operate at the same decision layer.
5. **One defensible key** — the selected answer best satisfies the stated factors.
6. **Contrastive rationale** — explains the key and why each plausible alternative
   fails the scenario constraint.

A question is **recall-heavy** when knowing one product fact is sufficient and the
scenario adds no decision-relevant information. Recall items are allowed when the
official task statement explicitly requires configuration or behavior knowledge, but
they must not dominate the readiness signal.

## Learning loop

Default loop:

1. Diagnostic retrieval.
2. Study only the material needed for the diagnosed gap.
3. Solve a new item.
4. Commit a short self-explanation before feedback.
5. Compare the explanation with all option rationales.
6. Solve a varied case on the same task statement.
7. Revisit misses after a delay.
8. Interleave similar concepts so the learner must discriminate between them.
9. Complete hands-on cases / capstone work.
10. Use held-out readiness forms for final calibration.

## Mastery policy

Foundation task-statement mastery requires repeated correct solutions to distinct
items; one correct response is not mastery. A later miss resets the current success
streak. Readiness additionally requires hands-on work and held-out evaluation.

Professional readiness additionally requires an end-to-end capstone that forces
architecture, integration, evaluation, governance, stakeholder, operational, and
handoff decisions to coexist in one system.

## Ten independent validation lenses

Each lens loads the corpus independently and does not consume another validator's
result.

1. Official scope and scenario alignment.
2. Cognitive demand: judgment vs recall.
3. Decision-hinge clarity and factor sufficiency.
4. One-best-answer / key defensibility.
5. Distractor plausibility and option homogeneity.
6. Cue resistance.
7. Contrastive rationale quality.
8. Transfer and case diversity.
9. Learning-loop quality.
10. Held-out readiness and calibration integrity.

Automated signals are review queues, not substitutes for expert review. Especially
for lenses 4 and 5, a stratified stress sample is manually adjudicated.
